The line that holds the primes

In 1859, a German mathematician named Bernhard Riemann spotted a hidden pattern in the prime numbers. He wrote it down as a guess. Then the world spent 167 years trying to prove it. In November 2026, a mathematician at Anthropic finally did, working side by side with Claude.

This is that story, told for people who don't do maths. Scroll down to follow it one chapter at a time.

  • Real part (σ) How far left or right. The zeros live between 0 and 1.
  • Imaginary part (t) How far up. It goes on forever. We show up to 42.
  • Height (3D view only) How big zeta's answer is. The floor means the answer is 0.
  • Critical line The line at ½. Riemann guessed every zero is on it.
  • Zero A spot where zeta's answer is 0. A gold bead marks each one.
Chapter 1

Primes look random

Our story starts with the primes. A prime is a whole number you can only divide evenly by 1 and itself: 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13… Every whole number is made by multiplying primes, so primes are the building blocks of all numbers. They even help keep your bank details safe online.

The trouble is, they show up with no clear pattern. Watch the scanner walk along: sometimes two primes sit side by side (11 and 13), and sometimes there is a long gap (none from 90 to 96). For centuries, people wondered: is there any order here at all?

Chapter 2

We can estimate the count. The question is how wrong the estimate can be.

The first clue came from a teenager. Around 1792, a young Carl Friedrich Gauss found a smooth curve, li(x), that estimates how many primes there are up to any number. It follows the real staircase of prime counts very closely.

But not perfectly. The real count wobbles above and below the curve. How big can that wobble get? That question is the heart of our story. The Riemann hypothesis says the wobble always stays small, inside a narrow corridor.

The lower chart zooms in on the wobble. The shaded band is the corridor the hypothesis promises (it applies from 2,657 onwards).

Chapter 3

Riemann turned the primes into a landscape

In 1859, Riemann found a way to see the wobble. He built a function called zeta: a kind of machine that holds information about every prime at once. You give it a spot on a map, and it gives back an answer. The 3D landscape at the top of the page shows how big that answer is at every spot.

Then Riemann went looking for the zeros: spots where the answer is exactly 0, where the landscape touches the floor. He worked out a few by hand and noticed something odd. They all sat on one straight line, exactly halfway between 0 and 1. It is called the critical line.

So he made a guess. The Riemann hypothesis: every zero, and there are infinitely many, is on that line.

The chart walks straight up that line. Every time the wave crosses the middle, you have found a zero.

Worked example: the first crossing is at t = 14.13, so the spot ½ + 14.13i is the first zero. The next crossings are at t = 21.02, 25.01, 30.42 and 32.94. That is exactly where the numbers like 14.13 on this page come from: nobody has a formula for them, so they are found by searching for the crossings.

Chapter 4

Inside the zeta machine

Before we go on, let's open up the zeta machine and see how it works. Click a frame to fly to it, then press Next. Arrow keys work too.

Zeta is a recipe

ζ(s) = Σ 1/nˢ = 1/1ˢ + 1/2ˢ + 1/3ˢ + 1/4ˢ + …

Pick a number s. Now go through the counting numbers n = 1, 2, 3, 4… That is every whole number, not only the primes. For each one, work out 1/ns ("1 divided by n to the power s"). Then add them all up, forever. The total is called "zeta of s", written ζ(s).

Other ways to write this
  1. Short, with Σζ(s) = Σ 1/nˢ
  2. Long, written outζ(s) = 1/1ˢ + 1/2ˢ + 1/3ˢ + 1/4ˢ + …
  3. With negative powersζ(s) = 1⁻ˢ + 2⁻ˢ + 3⁻ˢ + 4⁻ˢ + …1/nˢ and n⁻ˢ mean exactly the same thing.
  4. In wordsAdd up 1 divided by n to the power s, for n = 1, 2, 3, 4… and never stop.
  5. Example with s = 2ζ(2) = 1/1² + 1/2² + 1/3² + … = 1 + 1/4 + 1/9 + …

Zeta is built from primes

ζ(s) = Π 1/(1 − p⁻ˢ) = 1/(1 − 2⁻ˢ) × 1/(1 − 3⁻ˢ) × 1/(1 − 5⁻ˢ) × …

This second formula uses only the primes 2, 3, 5, 7… (here called p ) yet gives exactly the same answer as the first one. Why? Every whole number is made by multiplying primes in just one way (12 = 2 × 2 × 3). So when you multiply out the prime version, every piece 1/ns appears exactly once. That is why zeta knows about primes.

Other ways to write this
  1. Short, with Πζ(s) = Π 1/(1 − p⁻ˢ)
  2. Long, written outζ(s) = 1/(1 − 2⁻ˢ) × 1/(1 − 3⁻ˢ) × 1/(1 − 5⁻ˢ) × …
  3. Each bracket opened up1/(1 − p⁻ˢ) = 1 + 1/pˢ + 1/p²ˢ + …This is the "geometric series" rule: 1/(1 − x) = 1 + x + x² + x³ + …
  4. Why it matches the sum(1 + 1/2ˢ + 1/4ˢ + …)(1 + 1/3ˢ + 1/9ˢ + …)… contains 1/4ˢ × 1/3ˢ = 1/12ˢPick one piece from each bracket and multiply: you get 1/nˢ for one n. Every n turns up exactly once, because every number splits into primes in only one way.
  5. In wordsMultiply together one bracket for every prime 2, 3, 5, 7…

Use a spot on a map

s = σ + t·i

So far s was an ordinary number. Riemann let s be a spot on a map instead: σ (sigma) is how far across, t is how far up The i just marks the "up" direction

s = 2σ = 2, t = 0. Two across, nothing up: an ordinary number. With t = 0, no arrow turns.
s = ½ + 14.13iσ = ½, t = 14.13. Half across, 14.13 up. This is the first zero.
s = 0.75 + 5iσ = 0.75, t = 5. Three quarters across, 5 up. Not a zero.

Now each piece 1/ns becomes an arrow, built with two rules:

  1. Length: 1/n^σ. Later arrows are shorter.
  2. Direction: start pointing right, then turn clockwise by t × ln n. One full turn is 6.28.

Is t the same as i?

No. t is an ordinary number, like 5 or 14.13. The i is not a number you work out. It is a label that means "up", like the word "north" in "5 km north".

s = 0.75 (σ, across) + 5 (t, up) i (means up)

So 5i means 5 steps up. You can also write the spot as a pair, (0.75, 5), just like (x, y) on a graph.

Where does the i go? It does not disappear. To turn an arrow, t is used as a plain number: 5 × ln n says how much to turn. The i comes back in the answer: every arrow has an across part and an up part, and the up part carries the i.

1/n^(t·i) = cos(t ln n) − i·sin(t ln n)

cos and sin are calculator buttons that turn an angle into "how far across" and "how far up".

Worked example: s = 0.75 + 5i, arrow n = 2

  1. Size: 1 ÷ 20.75 = 0.59
  2. Turn: 5 × ln 2 = 5 × 0.69 = 3.47, which is 0.55 of a turn clockwise
  3. The arrow: 0.59 × (cos 3.47 − i sin 3.47) = -0.56 + 0.19i
  4. Read it: 0.56 to the left and 0.19 up. The 0.19 has the i on it, so it is the up part.

A small fix

1 − 1/2ˢ + 1/3ˢ − 1/4ˢ + … = (1 − 2¹⁻ˢ) × ζ(s)

Inside the strip (σ between 0 and 1), the arrows never settle down: the sum keeps wandering. Changing every other + into a − makes it settle. This new sum is 0 at exactly the same spots as zeta, so we can use it to find zeta's zeros.

Other ways to write this
  1. Short, with ΣΣ (−1)ⁿ⁻¹/nˢ
  2. Long, written out1 − 1/2ˢ + 1/3ˢ − 1/4ˢ + 1/5ˢ − …
  3. Its nameη(s) = (1 − 2¹⁻ˢ) ζ(s)Mathematicians call it the eta function, η(s). η is the Greek letter eta.
  4. How the sign flipper works(−1)⁰ = +1, (−1)¹ = −1, (−1)² = +1, …For n = 1, 2, 3, 4… the power n − 1 is 0, 1, 2, 3…, so the signs go + − + −.
  5. In wordsThe same pieces as zeta, but every second piece is taken away instead of added.

Where the fix comes from

1 − 1/2ˢ + 1/3ˢ − 1/4ˢ + … = (1 − 2¹⁻ˢ) × ζ(s)

  1. Start with zeta. Every piece has a + sign. The even pieces are shown in violet. ζ(s) = 1/1ˢ + 1/2ˢ + 1/3ˢ + 1/4ˢ + 1/5ˢ + 1/6ˢ + …
  2. Collect the even pieces. Every even number is 2 × n, and 1/(2n)s = 1/2s × 1/ns. So the even pieces are just zeta again, shrunk by 1/2s: 1/2ˢ + 1/4ˢ + 1/6ˢ + … = 1/2ˢ × ζ(s)
  3. Flip the even pieces from + to −. Taking them away once cancels them. Taking them away twice makes them negative: 1 − 1/2ˢ + 1/3ˢ − 1/4ˢ + … = ζ(s) − 2 × 1/2ˢ × ζ(s)
  4. Tidy up. Since 2 × 1/2s = 21−s: Σ (−1)ⁿ⁻¹/nˢ = (1 − 2¹⁻ˢ) × ζ(s)

Check with s = 2: both sides give the same number.

1 − ¼ + ⅑ − 1/16 + … = 0.8225… = (1 − ½) × 1.6449…

We did this where both sums settle (s bigger than 1). Mathematicians then show the same equation keeps holding inside the strip. The bracket (1 − 21−s) is never 0 there, so both sides are 0 at exactly the same spots.

The sum that gives 0

1 − 1/2ˢ + 1/3ˢ − … = 0 when s = ½ + 14.1347…i

Chapter 5

What does "zero" really mean?

Now we can answer a simple question. Zeta takes a spot s and gives back an answer. A zero is a spot where the answer is exactly 0. To find the answer, draw one arrow for each counting number n = 1, 2, 3… and put them end to end. Pick a spot and watch where the arrows stop.

Build it one arrow at a time

Every arrow follows three simple rules. Press Next arrow to watch one being made, or Play all to watch the whole chain.

  1. How long? Arrow n is 1 ÷ √n long. Arrow 1 is 1, arrow 4 is ½, arrow 100 is 1/10. Each one is a bit shorter than the one before.
  2. Which way? Every arrow starts pointing right, then spins clockwise by t × ln n. Here t is how far up the map our spot is: 14.13. And ln n is a slow counter: ln 1 = 0, ln 2 = 0.69, ln 10 = 2.30. So each new arrow spins a little more than the last. Why?
  3. Flip the even ones. Arrows 2, 4, 6… also turn round to face the other way. That is the minus sign in 1 − 1/2ˢ + 1/3ˢ − …, written (−1)ⁿ⁻¹ with Σ.

Why does each arrow point that way?

The length comes from σ, the across part. The direction comes from t, the up part. Here is why, in four steps.

  1. Split the power in two. Because s = σ + t·i, each piece breaks into an ordinary part and an i part: 1/nˢ = 1/n^σ (size) × 1/n^(t·i) (turn)You already know the size part. The turn part is the one with i in it.
  2. A power with i only turns. It never stretches. Remember, i means "one step up". Multiplying by i turns an arrow a quarter turn. Do it twice, i × i = −1, and the arrow points backwards: a half turn. So anything with i in the power is a turn, not a stretch.
  3. When numbers multiply, their turns add. 6 = 2 × 3, so the turn for 6 must be the turn for 2 plus the turn for 3. The ln button does exactly that: ln 6 = ln 2 + ln 3. That is why arrow n turns by t × ln n. Check it on the dial:
  4. t is the turning speed. With t = 0 nothing turns and every arrow points right. The bigger t is, the faster the arrows wheel round. The 1 over in 1/ns makes the turn go clockwise, and an angle of 2π ≈ 6.28 is one full turn.

Arrows n = 1 to 8, all drawn the same length so you only see their directions.

nln nt × ln nfull turns

The alternating version from Chapter 4 adds one extra half turn to the even arrows (2, 4, 6…). That is the flip. Why flip?

Worked example 1: arrow 3 for s = ½ + 14.13i, step by step

  1. Split the power into a size part and a turn part:1/3ˢ = 1/3^½ × 1/3^(14.13i)
  2. Length: 1 ÷ 3½ = 1 ÷ √3 = 0.58.
  3. Write 3 as a power of e. e ≈ 2.718 is a special number, and ln 3 is the power that turns e into 3:3 = e^(ln 3) = e^1.10
  4. Put that into the i part. The powers multiply:3^(14.13i) = e^(i × 14.13 × 1.10) = e^(i × 15.53)
  5. Euler's rule: ei × angle means "turn by that angle". Because the piece is 1 over it, the turn is clockwise: 15.53 ÷ 6.28 = 2.47 full turns clockwise.
  6. Result: arrow 3 is 0.58 long and has turned 2.47 times. In across-and-up numbers:1/3ˢ = -0.57 -0.10i0.57 left and 0.10 down.

You choose t. It is just a number, how far up the map you go. Any value works, and every spot gives an answer. Here are three tries, all with σ = ½:

Why do the even arrows flip?

Two reasons: the minus signs tell them to, and without the flips the arrows never settle down.

  1. A minus sign means "walk backwards". 5 − 2 is the same as walking 5 steps forward, then 2 steps back. So taking an arrow away is the same as adding that arrow turned round, half a turn.
  2. The even pieces have the minus signs. +1/1ˢ − 1/2ˢ + 1/3ˢ − 1/4ˢ + 1/5ˢ − … Pieces 2, 4, 6… are taken away, so arrows 2, 4, 6… are flipped. Odd arrows 1, 3, 5… are added just as they are.
  3. Why use minus signs at all? Inside the strip, zeta's plain sum (all plus signs) never settles: its chain keeps swinging round in bigger and bigger loops. Flipping every second arrow makes the chain settle down. It settles on 0 at exactly the spots where zeta is 0 (frames 7 and 8 of the walkthrough in Chapter 4 show why). Watch both below.

Chapter 6

Each zero is a note. Together they play the primes.

Here is why Riemann cared so much about the zeros. He found that you can rebuild the exact count of primes from them. Start with a smooth guess, then add one wave for each zero, like instruments joining an orchestra. The more waves you add, the closer you get to the exact staircase of primes.

Chapter 7

167 years of trying

Riemann died in 1866 without a proof. Over the next century and a half, many of the best mathematicians in the world tried. Here is why it was so hard.

∞

Infinitely many zeros

Computers have checked over ten trillion zeros, and all are on the line. But there are infinitely many. One stray zero anywhere would break the rule, and you can never check them all.

Σ 1/nˢ

The formula breaks where it matters

Zeta's simple sum only works to the right of the strip. Inside the strip, where the zeros are, mathematicians have to reach it by a roundabout route. That makes it much harder to study.

→ ½

Every method ran out of steam

Old methods could push zeros away from the edges of the strip, but they got weaker closer to the middle. For over a century, nobody could push all the way to the line.

$1,000,000

The most famous open problem

In 2000 it was named one of seven Millennium Prize Problems, with a $1 million prize. Most experts avoided it, because nobody could see a way in.

Chapter 9

Who did what

The proof came from a partnership. Levent Alpöge, a mathematician at Anthropic, chose the overall plan. He says he had been planning to tackle this problem since his student days. He started from a simpler practice version of the problem, then steered the work to the real one.

The method builds on an older trick by Stepanov, which solved a simpler cousin of the problem. That trick needs a property called multiplicity, which is very hard to get for ordinary whole numbers. Alpöge compared it to "differentiating whole numbers".

Claude's key idea: build that multiplicity from a chain of theta lifts, linked steps that each add a little more. Claude said the chain should be √X links long. Alpöge said this one idea would "make fellow mathematicians fall out of their chairs".

"Claude far outpaced me near the finish line. No, Claude flew, and danced, and sparkled near that finish line."Levent Alpöge, release note, 20 Nov 2026

Alpöge says there were tens of moments like this. Much of the proof uses advanced maths that he says he has "little mastery" of. Claude supplied that expertise.

Chapter 10

A proof no human has read, checked by machines

There was one catch: the proof is huge. It is written in Lean, a computer language where a machine checks every single step. Printed out, it would fill more than 250,000 pages, so only the code was released.

Could the computer be fooled? Only in two ways: the proof might answer a slightly different question, or the checker itself might have a bug. Within a day, experts had ruled out both.

"We hereby certify that both the statement and the proof of the Riemann Hypothesis are correct."Joint statement, Lean FRO and Mathlib Initiative, 21 Nov 2026

Chapter 11

Why it matters

So the guess Riemann wrote down in 1859 is now a fact. Here is what that changes, and what it doesn't.

"If" becomes "is"

Many results in maths began with "if the Riemann hypothesis is true…". They were stuck waiting. Now they are simply true.

Primes are as tidy as possible

The wobble in the prime count from section 02 is now proven to stay inside its narrow corridor. Primes look random, but they follow strict rules.

It goes even further

The proof also covers a bigger version, the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis (for "Dirichlet L-functions"). It is about primes in patterns, like primes that end in 1, 3, 7 or 9. One result: a fast, guaranteed test for whether a number is prime.

A "Rosetta Stone" for mathematics

"If the proof is correct and understood, this completes a dictionary between different branches of math where the connections were only partial." Hector Pasten, Pontifical Catholic University of Chile.

A turning point for AI

This is the first famous "impossible" maths problem solved with an AI doing much of the hard work. A computer checked it, so nobody has to take it on trust. "This is indeed an historic moment," said MIT's Andrew Sutherland.

What it does not do

It does not break encryption. As Scientific American put it, it "won't upend global banking or make the internet run faster". Most mathematicians already believed it was true. What is new is that we now know for sure.

Open questions

No one has written a readable paper yet. Some mathematicians worry that no human fully understands the proof. Michael Rubinstein is writing a 265-page guide to help, and parts of it still need filling in.

What comes next

Alpöge expects the proof to get much shorter soon, and plans to write it up properly. Now mathematicians get to understand why it works, and use its new ideas.

Symbol guide

Every letter and symbol on this page, in plain words.

n
The counting numbers 1, 2, 3, 4… Zeta's sum has one piece for every n, not only for primes.
p
A prime number: 2, 3, 5, 7, 11… Only used in zeta's second formula, the one built from primes.
s
The input you give zeta. A plain number like 2, or a spot on the map like ½ + 14.13i.
σ (sigma)
The across part of s, also called the real part. The zeros are all between σ = 0 and σ = 1. Small σ is not the same as big Σ below.
t
The up part of s, also called the imaginary part. t is an ordinary number you choose, such as 5 or 14.13; it never has the value i. Zeros happen only at special values (14.13, 21.02, 25.01…), found by searching.
i
A label for the "up" direction on the map, like "north". 5i means 5 steps up. Mathematicians defined it so that i × i = −1.
ζ(s)
"Zeta of s": the answer zeta gives for the input s. It is itself a spot on a map.
|ζ(s)|
The size of zeta's answer, meaning how far it is from 0. This is the height in the 3D landscape.
1/ns
One piece of zeta's sum: 1 divided by n to the power s. With s = 2 and n = 3 it is 1/3² = 1/9.
Σ
Big sigma: "add them all up". With n = 1 underneath and ∞ on top it means 1/1ˢ + 1/2ˢ + 1/3ˢ + … forever.
Π
Big pi: "multiply them all together". With "p prime" underneath, it means one bracket for each prime 2, 3, 5, 7…
∞
Infinity: keep going forever.
(−1)ⁿ⁻¹
The sign flipper: +1 when n is odd, −1 when n is even. It turns + + + + into + − + −.
zero
A spot s where zeta's answer is exactly 0. The first one is at ½ + 14.13i.
critical strip
The band of the map between σ = 0 and σ = 1. All the important zeros live in it.
critical line
The line down the middle of the strip, at σ = ½. The hypothesis says every zero is on it.
x
Just "some number" on the ordinary number line, such as "count the primes up to x = 1,000".
π(x)
How many primes there are up to x. Here π is only a name; it has nothing to do with 3.14159…
li(x)
Gauss's smooth estimate of π(x), the gold curve in Chapter 2.
ψ(x)
Another way of counting primes, used in Chapter 6. It also counts prime powers like 4, 8 and 9, which makes it easy to rebuild from the zeros.
e
A special number, about 2.718. Every positive number is a power of e: 3 = e1.10. That power is written ln 3. And ei × angle means "turn by that angle" (Euler's rule).
ln
The natural logarithm, a button on most calculators. It grows slowly: ln 10 ≈ 2.3, ln 1,000 ≈ 6.9.
√x
The square root of x: the number that times itself gives x. √100 = 10.
β (beta)
The across position of a rogue zero in Chapter 8. A zero at β makes a wave about as big as xβ.
